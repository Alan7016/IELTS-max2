Our Changing Climate: Causes and Consequences

The Earth's climate has always experienced natural fluctuations, but scientists are now in near-universal agreement that the rapid changes observed over the past century are primarily driven by human (1) activity. The burning of fossil fuels such as coal, oil and natural gas releases vast quantities of carbon dioxide and other greenhouse gases into the atmosphere, trapping heat and causing average global temperatures to (2) rise. This phenomenon, known as the greenhouse effect, has accelerated dramatically since the Industrial Revolution, transforming what was once a gradual natural process into an urgent planetary (3) crisis.

The consequences of climate change are already visible across the globe. Polar ice caps are (4) melting at unprecedented rates, contributing to rising sea levels that threaten low-lying coastal communities. Extreme weather events such as hurricanes, droughts and wildfires are becoming more frequent and more (5) severe, displacing millions of people and causing hundreds of billions of dollars in economic damage annually. Meanwhile, ecosystems that took thousands of years to develop are being disrupted faster than many species can (6) adapt.

Despite the urgency of the situation, international efforts to address climate change have been slow and frequently (7) inadequate. While the Paris Agreement of 2015 represented a significant diplomatic achievement, binding most nations to targets for reducing emissions, progress towards those targets has been inconsistent. Critics argue that without stronger enforcement mechanisms and greater financial commitment from wealthier nations, the world risks crossing irreversible (8) tipping points beyond which the damage to our planet will become impossible to undo.
