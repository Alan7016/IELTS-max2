Biodiversity Under Threat

The natural world is home to an estimated eight million species, yet scientists warn that we are currently living through the planet's sixth mass (1) extinction event. Unlike previous mass extinctions, which were triggered by natural catastrophes such as volcanic eruptions or asteroid impacts, the current crisis is driven almost entirely by human (2) behaviour. Habitat destruction, pollution, climate change and the overexploitation of natural resources have combined to create pressures that many species simply cannot (3) withstand.

Forests are among the most (4) biodiverse ecosystems on Earth, providing shelter and sustenance for an extraordinary range of plants, animals and insects. Yet deforestation continues at an alarming rate, with vast areas of tropical rainforest cleared annually to make way for agriculture, cattle ranching and commercial logging. When these habitats are destroyed, the species that depend on them often have nowhere (5) else to go, leading to rapid population decline and, in many cases, complete (6) extinction.

Marine ecosystems face equally severe challenges. Ocean (7) acidification, caused by the absorption of excess carbon dioxide, is bleaching coral reefs at unprecedented rates and disrupting the food chains that countless species depend upon. Meanwhile, overfishing has depleted fish populations across the world's oceans, threatening both the health of marine ecosystems and the food security of billions of people who rely on fish as their primary source of (8) protein.

Despite these alarming trends, conservation efforts offer genuine grounds for (9) optimism. Protected areas, rewilding projects and international agreements such as the Convention on Biological Diversity have demonstrated that it is possible to halt and even reverse species decline when sufficient political will and financial (10) commitment exist.
