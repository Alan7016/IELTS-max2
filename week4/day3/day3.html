The Politics of Climate Action: Why Progress Remains Elusive

Despite decades of scientific consensus regarding the severity of climate change, translating that knowledge into meaningful political action has proved extraordinarily (1) difficult. The fundamental challenge lies in what economists describe as a collective action problem: the costs of reducing emissions must be borne by individual nations and industries in the present, while the benefits — a more stable climate — are distributed globally and accrue primarily to (2) future generations. This misalignment of incentives creates powerful structural barriers to the kind of coordinated international response that the scale of the crisis demands.

The fossil fuel industry has historically played a significant role in (3) delaying progress. Through lobbying, the funding of climate scepticism and the cultivation of political relationships, major energy corporations have succeeded in weakening environmental legislation and maintaining subsidies that critics argue amount to state (4) support for one of the primary drivers of climate change. Governments in fossil fuel-dependent economies face particular pressure, as rapid decarbonisation risks unemployment and economic instability in (5) communities that have relied on these industries for generations.

International climate negotiations have achieved notable milestones, most significantly the Paris Agreement of 2015, in which nations committed to limiting global temperature rises to well below (6) two degrees Celsius above pre-industrial levels. However, these commitments are largely voluntary, and monitoring mechanisms remain insufficiently (7) robust. Critics point to the widening gap between the pledges nations have made and the actual emissions (8) reductions required to meet those targets.

Yet the picture is not uniformly bleak. The rapid decline in the cost of renewable energy technologies has begun to shift economic (9) incentives in ways that political advocacy alone could not achieve. Solar and wind power are now frequently the cheapest sources of new electricity generation in many markets, suggesting that the (10) transition to a low-carbon economy may ultimately be driven as much by commercial logic as by political will.

Answer — NO MORE THAN THREE WORDS from the passage:

1. What do economists call the problem where nations must bear costs now for future global benefits?
2. What have fossil fuel companies used to weaken environmental legislation?
3. What do critics say governments are providing to fossil fuel companies?
4. What does rapid decarbonisation risk in fossil fuel-dependent communities?
5. What was the most significant milestone of international climate negotiations?
6. How are the Paris Agreement commitments described?
7. What has declined rapidly to shift economic incentives toward renewables?
