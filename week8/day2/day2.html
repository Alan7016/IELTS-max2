The Prison System: Costs, Crises and Alternatives

Prison is the dominant response to serious crime in most countries, yet its effectiveness as an instrument of social policy remains deeply contested. The costs of (1) mass incarceration are enormous, both financially and in human terms. In the United States, the prison population exceeds two million people — the largest in the world in absolute numbers — at an annual cost running into tens of billions of dollars. In England and Wales, reoffending by ex-prisoners costs the economy an estimated eighteen billion pounds per year in crime, court proceedings and re-imprisonment.

Critics of the prison system argue that incarceration frequently makes things worse rather than better. Prisons expose first-time or minor offenders to experienced criminals, disrupt (2) family ties and employment, and create the conditions for radicalisation and the acquisition of criminal skills. Studies consistently show that short prison sentences, in particular, are among the least effective sanctions available — producing poorer outcomes on almost every measure than equivalent (3) community sentences, which keep offenders in society while requiring them to undertake unpaid work, attend programmes addressing their offending behaviour, or submit to electronic monitoring.

Several countries have experimented with radical approaches to reducing their reliance on imprisonment. Finland reduced its prison population by nearly two-thirds over several decades through a deliberate policy of (4) decarceration, combined with investment in welfare, education and drug treatment. Norway's prisons are designed around the principle of (5) normalisation — the idea that prison conditions should differ as little as possible from life outside, to preserve prisoners' capacity to function in society after release. The results are striking: Norway has one of the lowest (6) reoffending rates in the world, at around twenty percent, compared with figures approaching sixty percent in the United Kingdom and United States.

Defenders of imprisonment argue that it serves an irreplaceable function in protecting the public from dangerous individuals, and that the apparent success of Nordic models reflects cultural and social factors that cannot simply be transplanted elsewhere. The debate, however, has generated renewed interest in expanding the range of (7) sentencing options available to judges, with growing support for community-based, therapeutic and restorative alternatives to custodial punishment.

Reading Comprehension
Read five short passages about punishment systems and answer the questions

⚖ Passage A — The Death Penalty

Capital punishment — the execution of a person by the state as punishment for a crime — remains one of the most contentious issues in criminal justice. Around 55 countries retain the death penalty, including the United States, China, Iran and Saudi Arabia. Proponents argue that it serves as an ultimate deterrent against the most serious crimes, provides justice for victims' families, and permanently eliminates the risk of reoffending. Critics counter that there is no reliable evidence that capital punishment deters crime more effectively than long-term imprisonment, that it carries an irreversible risk of executing innocent people, and that it constitutes a violation of the fundamental human right to life. The global trend over the past century has been towards abolition: more than two thirds of the world's countries have now abandoned the practice in law or in fact.

1. What is the global trend regarding the death penalty over the past century?
2. What is one argument critics make against the death penalty?

⚖ Passage B — The Nordic Prison Model

Scandinavia — and particularly Norway — has attracted global attention for its approach to criminal justice. Norwegian prisons such as Halden and Bastøy are famous for their humane conditions: individual cells, access to education and vocational training, shared kitchens and communal spaces. The philosophy underpinning the system is that rehabilitation, not punishment, is the primary purpose of imprisonment. Prisoners are treated as people who will eventually return to society and need to be equipped to do so successfully. Norway's reoffending rate of around twenty percent contrasts sharply with rates of fifty to sixty percent in the UK and US. Critics, however, question whether this success can be attributed to the prisons themselves, or whether it reflects the broader social safety net, low inequality and high social trust that characterise Scandinavian societies more generally.

3. What is the primary philosophy behind the Norwegian prison system?
4. What concern do critics raise about attributing Norway's success to its prisons?

⚖ Passage C — Mandatory Minimum Sentences

Mandatory minimum sentencing laws require judges to impose a fixed minimum prison term for certain offences, regardless of the individual circumstances of the case. Introduced in many countries during the 1980s and 1990s as part of "tough on crime" political agendas, mandatory minimums were intended to create consistency in sentencing, deter offenders, and remove judicial discretion seen as leading to unduly lenient outcomes. Critics argue that such laws have contributed significantly to prison overcrowding, are racially disproportionate in their application, and prevent judges from tailoring sentences to the specific details of individual cases. Several US states and jurisdictions have begun to reform or repeal mandatory minimum laws following evidence of their unintended consequences.

5. What was a stated purpose of mandatory minimum sentencing laws?
6. What is one criticism of mandatory minimum sentences mentioned in the passage?

⚖ Passage D — Youth Justice

Young people who commit crimes pose a particular challenge for justice systems. Most jurisdictions treat juvenile offenders differently from adults, recognising that adolescent brains are still developing and that young people are more amenable to rehabilitation than adults with established criminal histories. Youth courts typically emphasise welfare and educational interventions over punishment, and many offences that would result in custody for adults are dealt with through cautions, referral orders or supervision in the community. However, the age at which young people can be held criminally responsible varies widely: from ten in England and Wales to fifteen in Norway. Critics of low ages of criminal responsibility argue that children this young cannot fully understand the consequences of their actions and that early contact with the justice system often accelerates rather than prevents a criminal career.

7. Why do most justice systems treat juvenile offenders differently from adults?
8. What concern do critics raise about low ages of criminal responsibility?

⚖ Passage E — Restorative Justice in Practice

Restorative justice programmes bring together offenders, victims and sometimes members of the wider community to discuss the impact of a crime and agree how it can be repaired. Unlike conventional court proceedings, which focus on establishing guilt and imposing punishment, restorative processes centre on dialogue, acknowledgement of harm, and the active participation of those most directly affected. Research evidence from multiple countries suggests that restorative justice, when delivered well, reduces reoffending more effectively than conventional sentencing, increases victim satisfaction, and reduces victims' experience of post-traumatic symptoms. Restorative approaches are most commonly used for less serious offences, though they are increasingly being piloted for more serious crimes, including cases of serious violence, where both parties consent to participation.

9. How does restorative justice differ from conventional court proceedings?
10. What does research suggest about the effectiveness of restorative justice?
