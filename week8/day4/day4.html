Crime, Media and Public Perception

The way crime is represented in the media has profound effects on public attitudes, policy preferences and political behaviour. Research consistently shows that media coverage of crime is systematically distorted: violent and sensational crimes receive disproportionate attention relative to their actual frequency, while everyday property crime — which makes up the vast majority of offences — is largely invisible. This distortion creates a phenomenon known as the (1) mean world syndrome — a term coined by sociologist George Gerbner to describe the tendency of heavy television viewers to perceive the world as more dangerous and threatening than it actually is.

The consequences extend beyond individual psychology. Public overestimation of crime rates consistently translates into demands for harsher sentencing, more aggressive policing and the expansion of prison capacity — regardless of whether crime is actually rising or falling. Politicians, acutely aware of this dynamic, often find it electorally advantageous to adopt (2) punitive positions on crime, pledging tougher sentences and crackdowns even when the evidence suggests that such measures are ineffective. This has been described as a (3) moral panic — a period of intense social concern about a perceived threat that is disproportionate to the actual level of danger.

The rise of (4) social media has intensified these dynamics. Crimes that would previously have attracted only local attention now achieve national or global visibility within hours, creating the impression of epidemic-level threat from relatively rare events. Simultaneously, social media platforms can accelerate the spread of (5) misinformation about crime — false reports, distorted statistics, and inflammatory framing that can inflame public opinion and complicate the work of the justice system.

Despite this, there is growing evidence that the public's relationship with crime news is more sophisticated than it might appear. Studies show that when people are given accurate information and time to reflect, their preferences for criminal justice policy become notably more (6) evidence-based and less punitive. This suggests that the gap between public opinion and effective policy may be at least partly a product not of genuine values but of (7) misinformation and the selective presentation of crime data.

⚖ Passage A — Knife Crime and Public Perception

In recent years, knife crime has dominated media headlines in the United Kingdom, prompting widespread public anxiety and urgent political debate. Statistics tell a more nuanced story. While recorded knife offences have risen in some periods, the long-term trend in overall violent crime in the UK has declined significantly since the early 1990s. Research by criminologists suggests that the increase in recorded knife crime partly reflects changes in police recording practices and the introduction of stop and search operations, rather than a genuine epidemic. Nevertheless, the issue has disproportionately affected young men in deprived urban areas — particularly Black communities — and has prompted calls for both tougher policing and investment in early intervention, youth services and education as preventive measures.

1. What does the long-term trend in overall violent crime in the UK show?
2. What does the passage suggest about the increase in recorded knife crime?

⚖ Passage B — The War on Drugs

The "war on drugs" — a term associated with a set of policies introduced in the United States under President Nixon in the 1970s and subsequently adopted in various forms across the world — has been one of the most controversial and heavily debated criminal justice strategies of the modern era. Proponents argue that strict prohibition and tough enforcement are necessary to protect public health and reduce drug-related crime. Critics, however, point to the failure of prohibition to reduce drug use, the enormous financial cost of enforcement and incarceration, the racially disproportionate impact of drug laws on minority communities, and the emergence of powerful criminal networks that profit from the illegal market. Several jurisdictions have experimented with decriminalisation or regulation of some drugs — most notably Portugal, which decriminalised personal possession of all drugs in 2001 and subsequently saw falls in drug-related deaths, HIV infections and drug-related crime.

3. What did Portugal do in 2001, and what were the results?
4. Which of the following is cited as a criticism of the "war on drugs"?

⚖ Passage C — Terrorism and the Law

Terrorism poses unique challenges for criminal justice systems designed around the prosecution of individual offenders for specific acts. Terrorist offences typically involve conspiracy, incitement and preparation for attacks that may never actually occur, making evidence gathering particularly difficult. Governments have responded by introducing special legislation extending police powers — including longer pre-charge detention periods, control orders restricting suspects' movements, and mass surveillance programmes. Critics argue that these measures erode fundamental civil liberties, are prone to misuse against innocent people — particularly from Muslim communities — and may be counterproductive by alienating the communities whose cooperation is most needed to identify and prevent extremism. The tension between security and liberty represents one of the defining legal and political debates of the early twenty-first century.

5. Why do critics argue that anti-terrorism measures may be counterproductive?
6. What makes gathering evidence in terrorism cases particularly difficult?

⚖ Passage D — Domestic Abuse and the Law

Domestic abuse — which encompasses physical, psychological, financial and sexual abuse within intimate or family relationships — has historically been treated as a private matter beyond the reach of criminal law. In many countries, it was not until the 1970s and 1980s that domestic violence was consistently criminalised and prosecuted. Even today, significant barriers to justice remain: many victims do not report abuse out of fear, financial dependence, shame or a lack of confidence that the justice system will respond effectively. The introduction of the Domestic Abuse Act 2021 in England and Wales represented a significant reform, introducing a new statutory definition of domestic abuse, extending the concept to include coercive control, and strengthening the powers of courts to protect victims. Despite this progress, conviction rates for domestic abuse remain lower than for many other serious offences, and support services for victims remain chronically underfunded.

7. What does the passage say about the historical treatment of domestic abuse by the law?
8. What was significant about the Domestic Abuse Act 2021?

⚖ Passage E — Environmental Crime

Environmental crime — including illegal dumping of toxic waste, wildlife trafficking, illegal logging and the trade in endangered species — has emerged as one of the fastest-growing categories of organised crime globally. Its consequences extend beyond environmental damage: wildlife trafficking, for instance, represents the fourth largest illegal trade in the world by value, generating billions of dollars annually for criminal networks and driving dozens of species toward extinction. Despite its scale, environmental crime is consistently underprioritised by law enforcement agencies, which tend to focus resources on crimes against persons and property. It is also notoriously difficult to investigate and prosecute, partly because it often crosses multiple national jurisdictions and partly because its victims — ecosystems, species populations, future generations — cannot speak for themselves in court. Growing calls for "ecocide" to be recognised as an international crime reflect the increasing urgency with which these issues are being treated by policymakers and legal scholars.

9. Why is environmental crime particularly difficult to investigate and prosecute?
10. What does the passage say about wildlife trafficking?
