Crime, Justice and Society

Crime is among the oldest and most persistent of social phenomena. Every known society has developed formal or informal systems to define which acts are prohibited, to identify those responsible, and to impose (1) consequences on those who violate collective norms. What counts as a crime, however, is neither fixed nor universal — it is shaped by history, culture, politics and power, and has shifted dramatically across time and place.

Modern criminal justice systems typically pursue several distinct goals, which are not always easy to reconcile. (2) Deterrence aims to discourage offending by making the anticipated costs of crime outweigh its benefits, either for the individual offender or for potential offenders in the wider population. (3) Rehabilitation takes a different approach, treating crime not primarily as a moral failing to be punished but as a behaviour rooted in social, psychological or economic circumstances that can, with appropriate intervention, be changed. (4) Retribution, meanwhile, holds that punishment is justified in its own right as a proportionate response to wrongdoing, regardless of its practical effects.

The relationship between poverty and crime has been extensively studied, though the nature of the relationship remains contested. Consistent evidence shows that individuals from economically deprived backgrounds are significantly overrepresented in prison populations across most countries, though researchers disagree about whether this reflects a genuine causal link between poverty and offending, or whether it is better explained by differential policing, (5) systemic bias within the justice system, and the limited resources available to low-income defendants to mount an effective legal defence.

In recent decades, many countries have experimented with alternative approaches to traditional (6) incarceration, including restorative justice programmes in which offenders meet with the victims of their crimes to take responsibility and agree reparations. Evidence suggests that such programmes can reduce (7) reoffending rates and increase victim satisfaction compared with conventional court proceedings, though they remain controversial and are not universally available.

1. What do societies impose on those who violate norms?
2. What approach aims to discourage offending by raising its costs?
3. What approach treats crime as behaviour rooted in circumstances that can be changed?
4. What holds that punishment is justified as a proportionate response to wrongdoing?
5. What does research suggest may explain overrepresentation of the poor in prisons?
6. What alternative to incarceration involves offenders meeting their victims?
7. What do restorative programmes appear to reduce compared with conventional courts?

Answers: 1-consequences, 2-deterrence, 3-rehabilitation, 4-retribution, 5-systemic bias, 6-restorative justice, 7-reoffending rates

⚖ Passage A — The Purpose of Punishment

Philosophers and criminologists have long debated why societies punish criminal behaviour. Three main theories dominate the debate. Retributivists argue that punishment is a moral necessity: wrongdoers deserve to suffer in proportion to the harm they have caused, regardless of whether punishment makes society safer. Consequentialists, by contrast, justify punishment only when it produces good outcomes — such as deterring future crime, incapacitating dangerous individuals, or rehabilitating offenders. A third view, restorative justice, focuses not on suffering but on repairing the harm done to victims and restoring social relationships. Each theory leads to very different conclusions about the appropriate form and severity of punishment.

1. What do retributivists believe about punishment?
2. What distinguishes restorative justice from the other two approaches?

⚖ Passage B — White-Collar Crime

White-collar crime — a term coined by sociologist Edwin Sutherland in 1939 — refers to financially motivated, non-violent offences committed by people in positions of trust, typically in business or professional settings. Examples include fraud, embezzlement, insider trading, money laundering and tax evasion. White-collar crimes often go undetected for years and can cause enormous financial harm to individuals, companies and governments. Despite this, perpetrators are statistically less likely to receive custodial sentences than street criminals. Critics argue that this disparity reflects the social and economic power of white-collar offenders, who are typically better resourced to mount an effective legal defence and who are judged by social peers more likely to be sympathetic to them.

3. Why do critics argue that white-collar criminals receive lighter sentences?
4. Which of the following is NOT mentioned as an example of white-collar crime?

⚖ Passage C — Prisons and Recidivism

High rates of reoffending — often called recidivism — represent one of the most persistent challenges facing criminal justice systems worldwide. In many countries, over half of all released prisoners are reconvicted within a few years of leaving custody. Research consistently identifies several factors that increase recidivism risk: lack of stable housing, unemployment, substance dependence, and weak social support networks. Effective post-release programmes addressing these issues — including supported housing, employment training, drug treatment and mentoring — have been shown to reduce reoffending significantly. However, such programmes require substantial investment and political will, and remain underfunded in many jurisdictions, where spending on punishment continues to far outweigh spending on prevention and rehabilitation.

5. What does the passage identify as a key factor increasing the risk of reoffending?
6. What does the passage suggest about spending on rehabilitation?

⚖ Passage D — Crime and Social Inequality

Criminologists have long noted a strong correlation between socioeconomic deprivation and crime. People from disadvantaged backgrounds are disproportionately represented both among those who commit crimes and among victims of crime. Several explanations have been proposed. Strain theory suggests that crime results from the tension between socially approved goals — such as wealth and status — and the limited legitimate means available to those at the bottom of the social hierarchy to achieve them. Social disorganisation theory links crime to the breakdown of community institutions, informal social controls, and collective cohesion in deprived urban areas. Others argue that apparent links between deprivation and crime are partly an artefact of selective law enforcement, in which poorer communities face heavier policing and stricter prosecution.

7. According to strain theory, why does crime occur?
8. What alternative explanation does the passage offer for the link between poverty and crime statistics?

⚖ Passage E — Cybercrime

The rapid expansion of digital technology has given rise to a new and rapidly evolving category of crime: cybercrime. This broad term encompasses offences ranging from hacking and identity theft to online fraud, ransomware attacks and the distribution of illegal content. Unlike traditional crime, cybercrime can be committed across national borders, often with near-total anonymity, making detection and prosecution extremely challenging. Victims range from individuals and small businesses to governments and critical national infrastructure. The global cost of cybercrime is estimated to run into trillions of dollars annually. Responses have included the development of specialist cybercrime units, international law enforcement cooperation, and increasing pressure on technology companies to improve digital security and cooperate with investigative authorities.
