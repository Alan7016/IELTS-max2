Policing, Evidence and the Courts

The criminal justice process begins long before a case reaches court. When a crime is reported or detected, police must gather sufficient (1) evidence to justify an arrest and, ultimately, a prosecution. This evidence may take many forms — witness statements, physical traces left at the scene, digital data, or the results of (2) forensic analysis conducted by specialists in pathology, toxicology or digital investigation. The standard of evidence required varies depending on the stage of the proceedings: police need only a reasonable suspicion to make an arrest, but a prosecution requires evidence sufficient to establish guilt (3) beyond reasonable doubt — the highest evidential standard in law.

Once charged, a defendant is entitled to legal representation. In most democratic systems, those who cannot afford a lawyer are provided with one at public expense through a system of (4) legal aid. The right to a fair trial — including the right to know the charges against you, to challenge the evidence presented, and to be presumed innocent until proven guilty — is enshrined in international human rights law and forms the foundation of modern criminal procedure.

In court, the prosecution bears the (5) burden of proof: it must demonstrate the defendant's guilt; the defence does not have to prove innocence. Evidence is tested through a process of examination and (6) cross-examination, in which lawyers from both sides question witnesses about their accounts. In jury trials, it is the jury — a panel of ordinary citizens — who ultimately decides whether the evidence is sufficient to support a conviction. In cases without a jury, that responsibility falls to the judge, who acts as (7) trier of fact as well as arbiter of law.

5. What must the prosecution demonstrate in court?
6. What process allows lawyers from both sides to question witnesses?
7. What role does the judge take on in cases without a jury?

Answers: 1-evidence, 2-forensic analysis, 3-beyond reasonable doubt, 4-legal aid, 5-(the defendant's guilt/) burden of proof, 6-cross-examination, 7-trier of fact

⚖ Passage A — The Role of the Police

The police serve multiple and sometimes conflicting functions in modern societies. Their most visible role is reactive: responding to reported crimes, gathering evidence, apprehending suspects and presenting cases to the prosecution. But policing also has a significant preventive dimension — uniformed officers patrolling communities, building relationships with residents, and intervening before crimes occur. In recent decades, models of community policing have emphasised the importance of public trust: research consistently shows that communities are more likely to report crimes and cooperate with investigations when they trust the police, and that heavy-handed or discriminatory policing tends to reduce this cooperation and undermine the effectiveness of law enforcement more broadly. The relationship between police and the communities they serve is therefore not merely an ethical question but a practical one with direct consequences for crime detection rates.

1. Why is public trust in the police important from a practical perspective?
2. What does the passage suggest is a consequence of heavy-handed policing?

⚖ Passage B — Miscarriages of Justice

A miscarriage of justice occurs when a person is wrongly convicted of a crime they did not commit. Such cases represent one of the most serious failures of any criminal justice system, and their consequences for the individuals concerned — who may spend years or decades in prison — are devastating. Among the most common causes of wrongful conviction are eyewitness misidentification, false confessions (often obtained under pressure or through coercive interrogation techniques), unreliable forensic evidence, and the concealment of exculpatory information by prosecutors. In recent decades, DNA evidence has played a transformative role in identifying wrongful convictions: in the United States alone, more than 375 people have been exonerated through DNA testing since 1989. These cases have prompted significant reforms to investigation procedures, interrogation practices and the rules governing disclosure of evidence to the defence.

3. What role has DNA evidence played in criminal justice in recent decades?
4. Which of the following is NOT mentioned as a cause of wrongful conviction?

⚖ Passage C — Organised Crime

Organised crime refers to coordinated criminal enterprises run by groups or networks for financial profit. Unlike opportunistic or impulsive offending, organised crime is characterised by structure, planning, division of labour and the use of violence or corruption to protect operations and eliminate competition. Major categories include drug trafficking, human trafficking, money laundering, illegal arms dealing and cybercrime. Organised crime groups often operate across national borders, exploiting differences in legal systems and the limited capacity of individual states to pursue investigations beyond their own jurisdiction. International bodies such as Interpol and Europol coordinate cross-border enforcement efforts, while instruments such as asset freezing and anti-money laundering legislation target the financial infrastructure that sustains criminal networks. Despite these efforts, the global revenues of organised crime are estimated to run into hundreds of billions of dollars annually.

5. What distinguishes organised crime from opportunistic offending?
6. What tools does the passage mention for targeting the finances of criminal networks?

⚖ Passage D — The Right to a Fair Trial

The right to a fair trial is one of the cornerstones of modern human rights law, enshrined in Article 6 of the European Convention on Human Rights and equivalent provisions in legal systems worldwide. Key elements include: the right to be informed promptly of any charges; the right to adequate time and facilities to prepare a defence; the right to legal representation, including free legal aid for those who cannot afford a lawyer; the right to examine witnesses and challenge the evidence against you; the right to an independent and impartial tribunal; and the presumption of innocence until proven guilty. Courts have repeatedly held that violations of these principles — such as trials conducted in secret, coerced confessions or the denial of legal representation — render any resulting conviction unsafe and liable to be overturned on appeal.

7. What does the presumption of innocence mean in practice?
8. What happens to a conviction obtained through violation of fair trial rights?

⚖ Passage E — Plea Bargaining

Plea bargaining is an agreement between a defendant and the prosecution in which the defendant agrees to plead guilty — either to the original charge or to a lesser offence — in exchange for a more lenient sentence or the dropping of additional charges. The practice is widespread in the United States, where the vast majority of criminal convictions result from guilty pleas rather than jury trials. Proponents argue that plea bargaining reduces the burden on courts, saves time and money, and gives defendants certainty about their outcome. Critics counter that it places undue pressure on innocent defendants to plead guilty rather than risk a heavier sentence at trial, that it removes public scrutiny from the justice process, and that it may result in inconsistent outcomes that undermine the principle of equal justice under law. Several countries that have traditionally avoided the practice are increasingly introducing it to manage growing court backlogs.

9. What is the main concern critics raise about plea bargaining?
10. Why are some countries introducing plea bargaining for the first time?
