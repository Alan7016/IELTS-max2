Rites of Passage: How Cultures Mark Growing Up

Across virtually every human society throughout history, certain key moments of transition from one life stage to another have been marked by elaborate ceremonies known as rites of passage. Anthropologist Arnold van Gennep, who first systematically studied these rituals in the early twentieth century, identified a remarkably consistent (1) structure underlying them, regardless of the specific culture involved: a separation from one's previous social role, followed by a transitional period, and finally a reincorporation into the community with a new status.

Coming-of-age ceremonies represent perhaps the most universally recognised category of rite of passage. In many Indigenous Australian communities, adolescent boys traditionally undergo initiation rituals involving physical endurance, isolation from the community and the transmission of sacred cultural (2) knowledge from elders. Similarly, the Jewish bar and bat mitzvah, the Latin American quinceañera, and the Japanese Coming of Age Day all serve a comparable social function: publicly announcing that a young person has acquired adult responsibilities and (3) privileges within their community.

What distinguishes a genuine rite of passage from an ordinary celebration, anthropologists argue, is the presence of a liminal phase — a period of ambiguous, in-between status during which the participant is neither fully a child nor yet fully an adult. This transitional state is often deliberately uncomfortable or (4) disorienting, intended to symbolically dissolve the old identity before the new one can be fully (5) established.

Modern industrialised societies, some scholars suggest, have largely lost many of their formal rites of passage, leaving young people without a clear, culturally sanctioned marker of when adulthood truly (6) begins. Milestones such as obtaining a driving licence, graduating from university or moving into independent housing have partially filled this gap, yet they typically lack the communal ceremony and symbolic intensity that traditional rites once provided. Some psychologists have speculated that this absence may contribute to the prolonged uncertainty many young adults today experience regarding their (7) identity and place within society.
