Artificial Intelligence and the Future of Work

The rapid advancement of artificial intelligence has prompted widespread debate about its long-term impact on employment. Unlike previous waves of (1) automation, which primarily displaced routine manual tasks, modern AI systems are increasingly capable of performing complex (2) cognitive functions — from analysing legal documents to diagnosing medical conditions — that were once considered the exclusive domain of highly trained professionals.

Economists are divided on the consequences. Optimists point to the historical precedent of technological revolutions: just as the industrial revolution ultimately generated new categories of employment even as it eliminated others, they argue that AI will similarly (3) spawn new industries and roles that we cannot yet fully envision. Pessimists, however, contend that the (4) unprecedented pace and scope of AI-driven disruption leaves insufficient time for workers and educational systems to adapt.

What is less disputed is that the impact will be deeply (5) uneven. Low-skilled workers in sectors such as manufacturing, retail and logistics are widely considered to be most at risk, while those with expertise in AI development, data science and creative problem-solving are likely to find their skills in growing (6) demand. This divergence threatens to widen existing socioeconomic inequalities unless governments intervene with targeted retraining programmes and updated (7) labour market policies.

Meanwhile, a growing number of organisations are exploring a more collaborative model, in which AI handles repetitive or data-intensive tasks while human workers focus on areas requiring empathy, ethical judgement and interpersonal communication — qualities that even the most sophisticated algorithms currently struggle to (8) replicate.

Answer — NO MORE THAN THREE WORDS from the passage:

1. What type of functions can modern AI now perform that professionals once handled?
2. What do optimists say AI will create, similar to past technological revolutions?
3. What do pessimists say there is not enough of for workers to adapt?
4. Which workers are considered most at risk from AI disruption?
5. What do governments need to provide to reduce inequality?
6. What qualities do humans have that AI currently struggles to copy?
7. What model are organisations exploring where AI and humans work together?

Answers: 1-complex cognitive functions, 2-new industries/roles, 3-(insufficient) time, 4-low-skilled workers, 5-retraining programmes, 6-empathy/ethical judgement/interpersonal communication, 7-collaborative model
