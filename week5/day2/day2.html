Globalisation and Business: A Double-Edged Sword

Few forces have reshaped the global business landscape as profoundly as (1) globalisation. Over the past several decades, falling trade barriers, advances in transportation and the rise of digital communication have allowed companies to source materials, manufacture products and sell to customers across vastly different markets, often with remarkable (2) efficiency. A smartphone designed in California might contain components from a dozen countries, assembled in another, and sold worldwide within weeks of production.

Proponents argue that globalisation has been overwhelmingly beneficial. By accessing larger markets, businesses can achieve economies of scale, lowering production costs and ultimately offering consumers lower prices and greater (3) choice. Multinational corporations bring investment, technology and employment opportunities to developing economies, while consumers everywhere gain access to goods and services that would once have been unavailable or prohibitively (4) expensive.

However, critics highlight significant drawbacks. Small local businesses frequently struggle to (5) compete with multinational corporations that benefit from superior economies of scale and marketing budgets. There are also concerns about the erosion of distinct local cultures and identities, as global brands and standardised products spread into every corner of the world, sometimes displacing traditional ways of life. Furthermore, the interconnectedness that globalisation creates means that economic shocks — whether a financial crisis or a pandemic — can spread rapidly across borders, affecting businesses and workers far from the original source of (6) disruption.

Perhaps most contentiously, globalisation has reshaped labour markets in complex ways. While it has lifted millions out of poverty in manufacturing hubs, it has also been blamed for job losses in industries that cannot compete with cheaper overseas (7) labour. Businesses today must therefore navigate not only the opportunities that globalisation presents but also the considerable risks and ethical questions that come with operating on a truly global (8) scale.

Answer — NO MORE THAN THREE WORDS from the passage:

1. What has globalisation allowed companies to do across different markets?
2. What can businesses achieve by accessing larger markets?
3. What do multinational corporations bring to developing economies?
4. What do small local businesses frequently struggle to do?
5. What can spread rapidly across borders due to interconnectedness?
6. What has globalisation been blamed for in industries that cannot compete?
7. What must businesses navigate when operating globally?

Answers: 1-source materials/manufacture products/sell to customers, 2-economies of scale, 3-investment, technology and employment, 4-compete (with multinational corporations), 5-economic shocks, 6-job losses, 7-opportunities and (considerable) risks
