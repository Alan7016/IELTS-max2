Behavioural Economics: Why We Make Irrational Financial Decisions

Traditional economic theory assumes that people are rational actors who make decisions designed to maximise their own financial wellbeing. Yet decades of research in behavioural economics have demonstrated that human decision-making is frequently shaped by psychological biases that lead to choices that are, by any objective measure, (1) irrational. Understanding these biases is increasingly important for businesses, policymakers and individuals seeking to make better financial decisions.

One of the most well-documented biases is loss aversion: the tendency for people to feel the pain of a financial loss far more intensely than the pleasure of an equivalent gain. This explains why investors often hold onto failing stocks far longer than is sensible, hoping to avoid (2) crystallising a loss, while simultaneously selling profitable investments too early to lock in gains. Businesses exploit this bias constantly, framing prices in terms of what customers stand to lose by not purchasing immediately, rather than simply what they might gain.

Another significant bias is present bias, or the tendency to overvalue immediate rewards relative to future benefits. This helps explain why so many people struggle to save adequately for retirement, despite intellectually understanding the long-term (3) consequences of failing to do so. The immediate gratification of spending money today consistently outweighs the abstract, distant benefit of financial security decades into the future.

Anchoring is a further psychological quirk that affects financial decision-making. When evaluating whether a price is fair, people tend to rely heavily on the first piece of information they encounter — an "anchor" — even when that figure is (4) arbitrary or irrelevant. Retailers frequently exploit this by displaying an inflated "original" price alongside a discounted price, making the sale price appear more attractive than it might otherwise seem.

Recognising these biases does not eliminate them entirely, but awareness can help individuals build (5) safeguards into their financial decision-making, such as automating savings contributions or imposing a mandatory waiting period before making significant purchases. Ultimately, behavioural economics suggests that improving financial outcomes requires not just better information, but better (6) systems designed around how humans actually think and behave, rather than how economic theory assumes they should.
