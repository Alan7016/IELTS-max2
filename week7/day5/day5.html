The Social Mind: A Psychology Review

The field of social psychology has produced some of the most illuminating and unsettling discoveries about human behaviour. What unites these discoveries is a single, counterintuitive insight: that human behaviour is far more shaped by (1) situational forces than people typically believe. We tend to attribute the actions of others to their character — a tendency known as the (2) fundamental attribution error — yet decades of research consistently show that context, social pressure and environmental cues are usually the stronger predictors of what people actually do.

The classic experiments of the twentieth century illustrated this with striking clarity. Milgram's obedience studies revealed that ordinary people could be led to cause harm simply by the presence of an authority figure issuing instructions. Asch's conformity experiments showed that even clear perceptual judgements could be overridden by group pressure. Darley and Latané's bystander research demonstrated that the (3) diffusion of responsibility in crowds could prevent help from being given even in urgent emergencies. In each case, the power of the situation overwhelmed individual moral intention.

More recent research has shifted attention inward, examining the psychological processes that operate beneath conscious awareness. Studies of (4) self-talk have shown that the inner voice — once dismissed as a trivial quirk — plays a significant role in regulating emotion, managing behaviour and supporting performance. Research on (5) habit formation has revealed that a large proportion of daily behaviour is not consciously chosen at all, but automatically triggered by environmental cues through a process involving the (6) dopamine reward system.

Taken together, these findings have a powerful practical implication. If our behaviour is so strongly shaped by forces outside our conscious control, then the most effective approach to changing it may not be willpower or moral resolve, but rather the deliberate redesign of our (7) social environment — changing the cues, contexts and social signals that trigger the behaviours we wish to encourage or discourage.

3. What psychological process in crowds prevents help being given?
4. What inner process plays a role in regulating emotion and supporting performance?
5. What does research show a large proportion of daily behaviour is shaped by?
6. What reward system is involved in automatic behaviour?

🧠 Passage A — The Obedient Brain

Stanley Milgram's 1961 obedience experiments remain among the most discussed in the history of psychology. Participants were instructed by a scientist in a white coat to administer what they believed were increasingly powerful electric shocks to another person. The "victim" was in fact an actor, and no real shocks were given — but participants did not know this. Despite audible expressions of pain and protest, around 65% of participants continued to the maximum shock level of 450 volts. Milgram concluded that ordinary people are capable of causing serious harm when placed under the authority of a legitimate-seeming figure, and when responsibility appears to be transferred to that figure rather than resting with the individual.

Subsequent research has qualified these findings. Conformity rates drop significantly when participants are given an ally — a single person who refuses to comply. This suggests that the pressure to obey is social as much as hierarchical: what people fear most is not the authority figure, but the isolation of standing alone.

2. According to the passage, what does the presence of an ally do to obedience rates?
3. What does the passage suggest people fear most in these situations?

🧠 Passage B — Hoarding and Hidden Pain

Hoarding disorder affects an estimated 2–5% of the population globally and is now recognised as a distinct mental health condition. For those who experience it, the accumulation of possessions is rarely about greed or laziness. Research consistently shows that hoarding often begins following significant personal loss — bereavement, trauma or relationship breakdown — and functions as a coping mechanism, providing a sense of control and security in an otherwise overwhelming world.

The consequences of hoarding can be severe: inaccessible living spaces, fire hazards, social isolation and, in some cases, eviction. Yet stigma remains one of the greatest barriers to seeking help. Many people with hoarding disorder report feeling deeply ashamed and reluctant to invite professionals or family members into their homes. Treatment approaches that emphasise compassion and gradual progress — rather than rapid clearance — have shown the greatest success in supporting long-term recovery.

🧠 Passage C — The Habit of Being Human

Habits account for a remarkable proportion of daily human behaviour — some estimates suggest over 40%. Unlike conscious decisions, habits are triggered automatically by environmental cues and reinforced by the brain's dopamine reward system. The cue triggers a routine, the routine produces a reward, and the reward strengthens the neural pathway so that the same cue is more likely to trigger the same routine in the future. This cycle — sometimes called the habit loop — operates largely below the level of conscious awareness.

Breaking an established habit through willpower alone is notoriously difficult precisely because willpower operates at the conscious level while habits do not. More effective strategies involve changing the environmental cues that trigger the habit, replacing the unwanted routine with a less harmful one that delivers a similar reward, or using life transitions — a move, a new job, a change in routine — to disrupt the existing cue-routine-reward associations before new ones become entrenched.

8. Why is willpower alone ineffective for breaking habits?

10. Which term from the passage describes the cue-routine-reward cycle?
