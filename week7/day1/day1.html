The Hidden Forces Behind Human Behaviour

For much of human history, we assumed that people make decisions based on logic and reason. The emerging field of behavioural science has overturned this assumption dramatically. Research now shows that the vast majority of human decisions are shaped not by careful deliberation but by (1) unconscious processes — mental shortcuts, emotional responses and deep-rooted social instincts that operate largely below the level of conscious awareness.

One of the most influential frameworks in behavioural science is the distinction between what psychologist Daniel Kahneman calls (2) System 1 and System 2 thinking. System 1 is fast, automatic and intuitive — it relies on (3) pattern recognition and emotional association rather than logical analysis. System 2 is slow, effortful and deliberate, engaged only when we encounter genuinely novel or demanding (4) problems. The trouble is that System 1 frequently produces errors — what scientists call cognitive (5) biases — that can lead even intelligent people to make poor decisions.

Among the most well-documented biases is confirmation bias: the tendency to seek out and remember information that confirms what we already (6) believe. Another is the availability heuristic, whereby people judge the likelihood of events based on how easily examples come to mind rather than on actual statistical probability.

Understanding human behaviour also requires acknowledging the extraordinary power of social influence. Classic experiments by Solomon Asch demonstrated that individuals would give clearly incorrect answers simply to (7) conform with those around them. Later work by Stanley Milgram showed that ordinary people would follow instructions from an authority figure even when those instructions seemed harmful — suggesting our behaviour is shaped far more by (8) context than by stable personality traits.
