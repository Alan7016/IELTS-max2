The Bystander Effect: Why We Don't Help

In 1964, a young woman named Kitty Genovese was attacked outside her apartment building in New York City. What made her case particularly disturbing to psychologists was not the attack itself, but the apparent failure of dozens of neighbours to intervene or even call the police. This case inspired one of the most important and troubling concepts in social psychology: the (1) bystander effect — the finding that individuals are less likely to offer help in an emergency when other people are present.

The psychological mechanisms behind the bystander effect are well understood. The first is (2) diffusion of responsibility: when multiple people witness an emergency, each individual feels less personally obligated to act, assuming that someone else will take charge. The second is (3) pluralistic ignorance: when people look to others around them for cues about how to respond and, seeing that others appear calm, incorrectly conclude that no emergency exists. Together, these processes can produce a paradox in which the (4) presence of others — which might seem to make help more likely — actually makes it far less probable.

Decades of research have confirmed the bystander effect across many different settings and cultures, though its strength varies considerably. In situations where the emergency is unambiguous, where the bystander has relevant skills or knowledge, or where the victim is clearly (5) identifiable as an individual rather than an anonymous member of a crowd, people are significantly more likely to intervene. Understanding that the bystander effect is a predictable psychological response — rather than evidence of personal (6) moral failure — may itself be one of the most effective tools for overcoming it.

Research also suggests a powerful antidote to bystander paralysis: (7) direct address. Experiments have shown that when someone in need specifically points to one individual in a crowd and asks for help, that person almost always responds. The personalisation of the request breaks the diffusion of responsibility and forces a specific individual to acknowledge their own (8) agency in the situation — transforming a bystander into a helper.
