Obedience, Conformity and the Social Self

Human beings are fundamentally social creatures. From the earliest stages of development, we learn to regulate our behaviour in response to the expectations of those around us, acquiring through this process an understanding of (1) social norms — the unwritten rules that govern acceptable behaviour within a given community or culture. The power of these norms is so pervasive that most people follow them not through deliberate choice but through an entirely (2) automatic process of social learning that begins in childhood.

The psychologist Solomon Asch demonstrated in a celebrated series of experiments during the 1950s just how powerfully social pressure shapes individual judgement. Participants were asked to compare the lengths of lines — a task so simple that errors would be almost impossible under normal conditions. Yet when surrounded by confederates who unanimously gave the wrong answer, approximately (3) thirty-seven percent of participants conformed to the incorrect group response, even though their own senses told them otherwise. Asch concluded that the desire to belong and avoid social (4) rejection is a powerful enough force to override even clear sensory evidence.

Stanley Milgram's obedience experiments, conducted in the early 1960s, produced findings that were even more disturbing. Participants were instructed by an authority figure to administer increasingly severe electric shocks to a person in another room. Despite audible signs of distress from the supposed victim, around (5) sixty-five percent of participants continued to the maximum shock level. Milgram interpreted these results as evidence not of individual cruelty but of the extraordinary human capacity for (6) obedience — a capacity that, he argued, is built into the very structure of hierarchical social organisation.

The concept of conformity extends beyond laboratory settings. Individuals regularly adjust their opinions, tastes and behaviours to align with perceived (7) group norms, often without consciously realising they are doing so. This process — sometimes called (8) normative influence — helps explain phenomena as diverse as the spread of fashion trends, the persistence of harmful group behaviours, and the difficulty individuals experience when trying to maintain minority opinions in the face of social pressure.
